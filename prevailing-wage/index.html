What is prevailing wage? How the rate is set, and who has to pay it

Checked 9 November 2026

Prevailing wage is the lowest hourly pay a law allows for a trade on public construction in one area. It comes in two parts, a base rate and a fringe (40 U.S.C. 3141(2)). On federal jobs the law is the Davis-Bacon Act, and the U.S. Department of Labor sets the rates. Many states have their own law for state-funded work, and Michigan's came back in 2024.

  • The federal law covers contracts over $2,000 to build, alter or repair public buildings and public works, painting and decorating included (40 U.S.C. 3142(a)).
  • DOL sets a rate for each trade, for each area and type of construction. If more than half the workers in a trade got the same wage on similar projects, that wage prevails (29 CFR 1.2).
  • Michigan sets its state rates from the collective bargaining agreements in each area (MCL 408.1104). LEO's rates page says they're updated every year on 1 November.

Here for an H-1B or PERM filing? That prevailing wage is a separate DOL program. Employers ask DOL's National Prevailing Wage Center for it on Form ETA-9141. This page is about construction.

How DOL decides which wage "prevails"

The federal test is written in 29 CFR 1.2. For each trade, DOL looks at what workers were paid on similar projects in the area. Then it uses the first of these that fits:

  1. A majority. If more than half were paid the same wage, that wage prevails.
  2. The biggest group, if it's 30 percent or more. If no wage has a majority, the wage paid to the largest group prevails, as long as that group is at least 30 percent of the workers.
  3. A weighted average. If no wage reaches 30 percent, the prevailing wage is the average of all of them, weighted by how many workers got each one.

  • Survey 1: no wage has a majority, since the largest group is 8 of 20, or 40 percent. But 40 percent clears the 30 percent bar, so that group's $31.00 prevails.
  • Survey 2: the largest groups are 5 of 20, or 25 percent each, so no wage reaches 30 percent. The prevailing wage is the weighted average: 5 × $32.00 + 5 × $30.00 + 4 × $28.00 + 4 × $27.00 + 2 × $25.00 = $580.00, and $580.00 ÷ 20 = $29.00.

In survey 2 nobody was paid $29.00. An average can land on a rate no one in the area actually gets.

Where DOL gets its numbers

Contractors, contractor groups, unions, public officials and others send DOL the wages paid on construction projects in the area. DOL also looks at signed collective bargaining agreements and at rates that state and local governments set (29 CFR 1.3). For building and residential rates, it leaves out pay on federal and federally assisted projects unless there isn't enough other data. For heavy and highway rates, it uses that pay too (29 CFR 1.3(d)). It can also adopt a state's or city's rate, if that government's process passes four tests, one being that its criteria are "substantially similar" to DOL's (29 CFR 1.3(g) and (h)).

Each rate holds for an area and a type of construction. The area is the city, town, village, county or other civil subdivision where the work happens. Highway work can use a state's highway districts, and a project in several counties can use all of them. The types include building, residential, heavy and highway (29 CFR 1.2). That's why one county can have several wage determinations at once. How to find yours and read its rate lines.

A job in Michigan can fall under the federal law, the state law, or neither. If a state project's contract already requires federal Davis-Bacon rates, Michigan's act doesn't apply to it (MCL 408.1102(1)).

Other states have their own laws, and DOL's list is behind

DOL keeps a table of state prevailing wage laws and the contract size where each one starts. Its table lists 28 states and Washington, DC, and its list of states without a law names 22. The starting points run from no minimum at all in Illinois, Massachusetts and New York to $1,000,000 for new construction in Connecticut.

Read that table with care. DOL last revised it on 1 January 2023, and it still lists Michigan as "repealed in 2018". Michigan's new act has been in effect since 13 February 2024. The page itself tells readers to check with the state's labor office, so do that for any state before you bid.

In other states, a federal job may owe both. DOL's Fact Sheet #66 says work under the federal rules may also be subject to state or local prevailing wage and overtime rules.

Questions

Prevailing wage questions

What is prevailing wage?

The lowest hourly pay a law allows for a trade on public construction in one area, in two parts: a base rate and a fringe. On federal jobs the law is the Davis-Bacon Act, and the U.S. Department of Labor sets the rates.

How is the prevailing wage worked out?

DOL looks at what workers in a trade were paid on similar projects in the area. A wage paid to more than half of them prevails. With no majority, the wage paid to the largest group prevails if that group is at least 30 percent. Otherwise it's the average of all of them, weighted by how many workers got each one.

Is the H-1B prevailing wage the same thing?

No. That's a separate DOL program: employers ask DOL's National Prevailing Wage Center for it on Form ETA-9141. This guide is about construction pay.

Does Michigan have a prevailing wage law?

Yes. Michigan's new act has been in effect since 13 February 2024. The state sets its rates from the collective bargaining agreements in each area, and LEO's rates page says they're updated every year on 1 November.

Can a job owe both federal and state prevailing wage?

It can. DOL's Fact Sheet #66 says work under the federal rules may also be subject to state or local prevailing wage and overtime rules. In Michigan, a state project whose contract already requires federal Davis-Bacon rates isn't covered by the state act.

Which states have a prevailing wage law?

DOL's table lists 28 states and Washington, DC. It was last revised on 1 January 2023 and still lists Michigan as repealed, so check with the state's labor office before you bid.
